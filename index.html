Module 01 · Intro to Visuals in AI

Somebody tagged this, before any model saw it.

Every photo a vision model learns from picked up a label somewhere along the way — alt-text typed by a stranger for accessibility, a bounding box drawn by a paid annotator, a caption written by another AI. This module opens the field notes on how that annotation actually happens, and follows one batch of images from a public webpage all the way to a deployed, fine-tuned model.

  • 01 Trace visual annotation from hand-labeled digits to today's AI-written captions
  • 02 Follow an image's full journey from a scraped webpage to a deployed model
  • 03 Pick up the shared vocabulary used across data, pretraining, and alignment teams

Lifecycle of visual data: scraping to feedback

Drag the small photo down the winding path (or use the button below) to carry one image through the full pipeline. Each stage unlocks as the photo reaches it — open an unlocked stage for the details.

01ScrapingDATA
Billions of images and their surrounding text — including alt-text — get pulled from the open web, usually via Common Crawl, producing raw image-text pairs. Nothing here has been checked for quality, accuracy, or safety yet. Source: Schuhmann et al., LAION-5B
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
02FilteringDATA / SAFETY
Deduplication strips near-identical copies; low-resolution images are dropped; pairs where the image and text don't really match are scored out — often using CLIP itself as the judge. NSFW classifiers and other safety filters flag unsafe content for removal. Source: Schuhmann et al., LAION-5B (filtering pipeline)
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
03Labeling & annotationDATA
What survives filtering gets annotated: some images by paid human annotators drawing bounding boxes or segmentation masks — with ground-truth checks to keep quality up — and, increasingly, a large share captioned by an existing capable AI model, since raw alt-text is short, inconsistent, or simply wrong. Sources: devopedia.org, ImageNet annotation process · "From Pixels to Prose"
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
04Pre-trainingMODEL
An image encoder — often a Vision Transformer — and a text encoder are trained together with a contrastive learning objective, or the image encoder is plugged into a large language model, so pictures and words share one embedding space. Sources: Radford et al., CLIP · Dosovitskiy et al., ViT
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
05Supervised fine-tuningTRAINING
The pretrained model goes through fine-tuning on carefully written instruction-and-response examples so it learns to actually answer questions about images helpfully — not just associate pictures with text in the abstract.
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
06Preference-based post-trainingTRAINING
People — or increasingly another AI model — compare pairs of the model's answers and say which is better. That preference data trains a separate reward model that learns to predict what a rater would prefer. Source: Christiano et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences"
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
07RLHF / RLAIFTRAINING
The model is optimized against the reward model, nudging it toward the kinds of responses raters preferred — that's RLHF. RLAIF swaps in AI-generated preference judgments to run this step at far greater scale than human review alone allows. Source: Ouyang et al., InstructGPT
🔒 MOVE THE PHOTO HERE
08Deployment & real-world feedbackLOOP
People actually use the model. Thumbs up/down, corrections, and reported failures get collected and, after review, some of that becomes new training or annotation data — which flows back into filtering and labeling for the next round.

The flywheel: real usage generates the annotation needs — and the filtering questions — that reshape stages 02 and 03 for the next training run. Visual AI pipelines rarely run once; they cycle.

QUESTION 1 / 3
Which came first in the history of visual annotation?
Not quite — check the timeline dates and try another.
✓ ImageNet moved to crowdsourced labeling on Mechanical Turk in 2008 — CLIP's web-alt-text approach came in 2021, and synthetic captioning took off after that.
QUESTION 2 / 3
In RLAIF, who provides the preference judgments used to train the reward model?
Not quite — the "AI" in RLAIF is the clue.
✓ RLAIF swaps human raters for an AI model that supplies the preference judgments — trading some human oversight for much greater scale.
QUESTION 3 / 3
Why do modern pipelines increasingly generate synthetic captions instead of using scraped alt-text directly?
Not quite — think about the quality of alt-text, not its availability.
✓ Alt-text exists and is machine-readable — the problem is quality. It's written for accessibility, not training, so it's frequently too short or just wrong. A capable model can write denser, more accurate captions.
